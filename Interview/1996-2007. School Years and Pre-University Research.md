### 30-Second Summary

Eleven years at School No. 132 in Minsk, with the period from the end of school year nine through graduation documented here. From May 2005 to 2007 I travelled weekly to the Faculty of Chemistry at Belarusian State University, where our school's ecological club prepared research work at the Department of Analytical Chemistry under Professor S. A. Mechkovsky. The subject was sorbent systems for monitoring water bodies - fieldwork on the Svislach river in Minsk, ionometric and chromatographic measurement, and the ion-exchange capacity of plant materials as low-cost sorbents. It produced three papers and was presented from district level up to an international conference of young scientists at the National Academy of Sciences of Belarus. It ended with entry to the radiophysics programme at BSU in September 2007.

### Two-Minute Overview

School No. 132 is an ordinary Minsk secondary school in the Frunzenski district, and for most of the eleven years there is nothing to report beyond it. What matters is the last two years, and the thing that made them different was the school's ecological club, "White Swan".

The club did two things at once. One was conservation work in the field, under the national bird-protection organization APB - BirdLife International's partner in Belarus - which is where the 2006 diploma from the EU-experience TACIS project comes from. The other was research: every week, from the end of school year nine through the final two full school years, we went to the Faculty of Chemistry at Belarusian State University and worked at the Department of Analytical Chemistry under Professor S. A. Mechkovsky. Three of us from the school took part - myself, Aliaksandr Malashkevich and Dzmitry Titou.

The subject was sorbent systems, approached from two sides. The first was a survey of the Svislach river where it runs through Minsk: water and bottom-sediment samples from five points along the river, compared against an artesian source in the Vesnianka district and against the "Lebiazhy" reserve, characterized across six indicators - pH, total salt concentration, chloride content, carbonate and total hardness, protolytic buffering capacity, and the ability of the sediments to inactivate heavy metals. The second was a laboratory study of plant materials - roots, leaves and seed coats of plants grown on the sod-podzolic loam typical of Belarus - measured as cation exchangers by potentiometric titration, to see whether cheap, naturally renewable phytosorbents could stand in for synthetic ion-exchange resins.

That work was written up three times: a full paper with an appendix of measurement data, an English version of it, and a shorter joint paper with both co-authors. It was presented at the Frunzenski district schoolchildren's conference, at the XXV Minsk city conference, at a BSU students' conference, and at the international conference of young scientists "Youth in Science - 2006" at the National Academy of Sciences of Belarus. In February 2007 it reached the final of the Belarusian stage of the International Water Prize.

Six months later I entered the radiophysics programme at BSU. The subject changed completely; the habit of taking a question through measurement, analysis and written defence did not.

## 02 Context and Scope

### The Documented Part and the Undocumented Part

The repository holds nothing from 1996 to 2004. Those are the first nine school years, and there is no certificate, paper or record of them worth stating. The documented period is 2005 to 2007 - school years nine, ten and eleven - and everything in this document belongs to it. The date range in the title covers the whole of school because that is the period the document is about; the evidence covers the end of it.

### The "White Swan" Ecological Club

The club was based at School No. 132 and it is the thread that ties the rest together. Two strands ran through it.

**Conservation work.** The club took part in the field programme of APB-BirdLife Belarus, the public organization that was BirdLife International's national partner in Belarus. Under the TACIS project "Introducing European Union experience in the conservation of nature reserves and sanctuaries jointly with the local population and authorities", run with the RSPB, the club worked on the "Wild Square" fauna and flora survey programme and on conservation actions. The honorary diploma of 1 November 2006, signed by the APB chairman U. V. Ivanouski, is for that work rather than for the chemistry.

**Correspondence beyond Belarus.** The club's conservation work drew attention outside the country; at one point it received a letter from Brigitte Bardot, whose foundation works in animal protection. It is a small thing, but it is the kind of small thing that tells you what the club actually was: not a school activity on paper, but a group doing work that people outside the school noticed.

The research described below was prepared within the same club. That is the practical reason a school pupil had a desk at a university department: the club had the arrangement, and the club is what brought us there every week.

### How a School Pupil Ended Up in a University Laboratory

The Department of Analytical Chemistry at the BSU Faculty of Chemistry ran research work with school pupils, and Professor S. A. Mechkovsky supervised ours. Three of us from School No. 132 joined in May 2005, near the end of school year nine, and went there weekly through graduation in 2007. The department supplied the instruments, the methods and the supervision; the measurements, the analysis and the writing were ours.

That arrangement is why the record looks the way it does. The conference trail runs through the school system - district and city conferences of schoolchildren, because that is the category we competed in - while the work itself was done on university equipment and was also presented at a BSU students' conference and at the Academy of Sciences.

### Scope of the Work

- **Field survey.** Five sampling points along the Svislach within the city, plus two reference sources outside it.
- **Laboratory measurement.** Ionometry, potentiometric titration, frontal chromatography.
- **Materials study.** Plant materials from typical Belarusian species, characterized as ion exchangers.
- **Written output.** Three papers, one of them in English, one of them jointly authored.

### Constraints

- School-level access: the instruments, the reagents and the supervision came from the department, and the scope of the work was set by what a pupil could reasonably run there.
- No independent reference data: there was no external dataset against which to check the river measurements, so the design compensated with reference sources - an artesian well and a protected reserve - measured on the same indicators by the same methods.
- Time: the work ran weekly alongside the last two years of school and the entrance examinations that closed them.

## 03 Methods and Instruments

### Water and Bottom Sediments

| Indicator | Method | Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| pH of water and of aqueous extracts of bottom sediments | Direct potentiometry | Glass indicator electrode with silver-chloride reference; I-130 laboratory ionometer, precision +/-0.01 pH |
| Redox properties | Direct potentiometry | Platinum electrode against silver-chloride reference |
| Total salt concentration and ion composition | H-cationite followed by pH-metric completion, by the titration-curve method | Same electrode pair |
| Chloride content | Potentiometric titration | Silver electrode |
| Ability of sediments to inactivate heavy metals | Frontal chromatography through a column packed with the sediment under study, with chromato-titrimetric determination of Cu(II) in the eluate | Column and titrimetry |

### Plant Materials as Ion Exchangers

Samples were taken in August and September 2005 from different parts of plants grown on the sod-podzolic loam characteristic of Belarusian landscapes, then washed, dried and ground in an agate mortar to below 0.1 mm. Weighed portions of 0.5 g, to +/-0.0001 g and corrected for moisture, were equilibrated with an electrolyte background of 20 ml 0.1 M NaCl or 0.05 M CuCl2 and titrated with 0.1 M HCl and NaOH in doses of 0.20 to 1.00 ml, with pH read after each dose. Titration curves were plotted in pH-V coordinates, protolytic capacity computed as Z = cV/m, and Bronsted pK spectra derived from the Henderson-Hasselbalch relation.

### Why the Design Looked Like That

Two choices carried the work. Measuring reference sources on the same indicators as the river turned a set of absolute numbers, which nobody could interpret, into differences that could be argued about. And running the phytomaterial titrations on two electrolyte backgrounds - plain NaCl and CuCl2 - meant the effect of copper ions on exchange capacity came out of the same experiment rather than requiring a second one.

## 04 Findings

- pH across all water samples varied narrowly, from 7.2 to 7.9 with a mean of 7.6, which rules out any discharge in Minsk substantially shifting the river's acid-base characteristics.
- Total salt concentration differed materially between sampling points; surface runoff was inferred as the principal salt source rather than point discharges.
- Chloride concentration was highest in the water of the "Lebiazhy" reserve.
- Silicate ions appeared at the Vesnianka and Gorky Park points, attributable to the geological conditions of water formation and to wash-off from street and building surfaces.
- Bottom sediments from the Svislach showed the highest capacity to inactivate heavy metals of the materials tested.
- All plant materials studied proved to be cation exchangers with high exchange capacity; red clover roots, bean sprouts, ten-day rye roots and the leaves of mustard, potato and chestnut stood out.
- Exchange capacity was highest in the acidic region, pH 2-4, lower in the alkaline region, pH 9-11, and lowest near neutral, pH 4-9.
- Against a CuCl2 background, total capacity over pH 2-7 reached 4.54, 5.48 and 3.94 mmol-eq/g for potato, mustard and chestnut leaves - comparable with sulfonate cation exchangers at about 5.0 mmol-eq/g, against 8-10 for carboxylic ones.
- The materials were polyfunctional, with acidity constants spanning pK 2 to 12, which is what makes them usable across different applications rather than at one pH.

## 07 Why This Period Is in the Set at All

It earns its place for three reasons and no more than three.

**It is where research work started.** The first paper, the first conference talk, the first time a measurement had to be defended in front of people who could challenge it - all of that is 2005-2007, three years before the university coursework that is usually cited as the beginning. And it was weekly, not occasional: two school years of turning up at a university department.

**It explains the choice of degree.** The pattern that runs through everything after - instruments, measurement, turning a physical quantity into a number somebody can act on - is visible here in an ionometer and a titration curve before it appears in a spectroradiometer, a satellite payload or a laser scanner. First place in the school physics olympiad in 2007 and enrolment in radiophysics that September are the same decision seen twice.

**It is the first work in English.** Translating one's own paper at sixteen is a small thing, but it is where technical English began.

It does not earn a place as engineering experience, and it is not offered as one. In an interview it is an answer to "how did you get into this", not a line on the CV.

### Glossary

- **Sorbent:** a material that binds dissolved substances onto its surface, concentrating them from a solution.
- **Phytosorbent:** a sorbent made from plant material rather than synthesized - cheap, renewable and biodegradable.
- **Ionometry:** measuring the concentration of a specific ion through the potential of an electrode sensitive to it.
- **Potentiometric titration:** following a reaction by the change in electrode potential rather than by a colour indicator, which gives a curve rather than a single endpoint.
- **Ion-exchange capacity:** how much of an ion a material can take up in exchange for another, in millimole-equivalents per gram - the figure that decides whether a sorbent is worth using.
- **Frontal chromatography:** passing a solution continuously through a packed column and measuring what emerges, used here to see how much copper the sediments retained.
